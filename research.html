Working Papers

  1. The Daughter Effect in Politics: Evidence from Japanese National Legislators

    with Taketo Hara, Ritsu Kitagawa, and Takeru Sone. March 2026. [Working paper]

    Abstract

    We examine how politicians’ family structure shapes policy preferences, focusing on the influence of daughters on attitudes toward gender-and family-related policies. Using original data on the number and gender of children of candidates elected to Japan’s House of Representatives in 2021, combined with candidate survey responses collected during the election campaign, we analyze whether exposure to daughters affects positions on optional separate surnames for married couples, same-sex marriage, and legislation promoting understanding of sexual minorities. Exploiting plausibly exogenous variation in the number of daughters conditional on the number of children, we find that daughters are associated with more progressive policy positions among male candidates. Our findings highlight the role of family-based socialization in shaping elite political preferences and extend evidence on the “daughter effect” to a non-Western context.

  2. Height Premium Inside the Firm: Evidence from Personnel Records

    with Ritsu Kitagawa.

    Abstract

    Taller individuals tend to earn more, but less is known about whether this association persists within a common personnel system. This paper studies the height premium inside a modern firm using linked personnel and health-check records from a large Japanese manufacturing company. The data relate employees’ height to compensation, subjective evaluations, pay grade, and managerial attainment over nearly two decades. Among male employees, height is positively associated with gross pay, base salary, bonuses, pay grade, and managerial status. A 10 cm increase in height is associated with a 1.4 percent increase in gross pay, a 0.118-unit increase in pay grade, and a 4.1 percentage point increase in the probability of being a section manager. The gradient is weaker and less precisely estimated among female employees. The association is particularly pronounced in sales and is often asymmetric, with shorter men receiving lower evaluations and being less likely to attain managerial positions. These findings suggest that the height premium appears not only across firms or occupations, but also within the evaluation, grading, and promotion processes of an internal labor market.
